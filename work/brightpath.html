Brightpath

The problem

Most learning apps assume a child who reads English fluently, a fast connection and a new phone. Many children in Zimbabwe have none of those, and children who learn differently are the first to be left behind when an app moves at its own pace instead of theirs.

What I built

Brightpath is a set of maths and reading games in Shona and English. Every question is read aloud, with the other language one tap away. Teachers and parents get their own dashboards.

  • Pathfinder, the adaptive engine, keeps a mastery rating for each skill and picks the level where a child succeeds most of the time without it being too easy. It is plain, unit-tested logic with no AI calls.
  • A hint ladder: a wrong answer is diagnosed (for example, adding instead of subtracting), and the child stays on the same question with a nudge, then worked steps, then a guided walkthrough.
  • The server regenerates each question from its seed and grades it, so results cannot be faked from the phone.
  • Offline first: games are cached on the phone, and answers given offline queue and sync when the connection returns.
  • An opt-in Shona pronunciation check that scores a recording in memory and never stores it.
  • Teacher tools: learning plans with measurable goals, assigned practice, and a short check-in that is clearly labelled as not a diagnosis.

What happened

Brightpath grew out of LetterLight, an earlier prototype for dyslexic and neurodivergent learners that was piloted with children at John Cowie Primary School in Rusape. The project was also entered in the 2026 AI Case Innovation Competition run by the Embassy of China in Zimbabwe, and passed the preliminary review.

Brightpath is the rebuild that came out of that work. It is live as a web demo and as an Android app that opens it full screen. The Shona voice lines were generated with an open speech model and still need review by a native speaker, which is the next step before wider classroom use.
